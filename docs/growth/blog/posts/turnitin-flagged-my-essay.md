# Turnitin flagged my essay as AI. What now?

*The score is a guess about your words, not evidence about you. Turnitin says so itself.*

If Turnitin flagged your essay as AI and you wrote it yourself, the most useful thing to know is that the score isn’t an accusation. It’s a prediction. Turnitin’s own reports say the score may misidentify human writing and shouldn’t be the sole basis for action against a student.[1]

So the question in front of you isn’t “how do I argue with a number?” It’s “how do I show how I wrote this?” That’s a much better question, because it has an answer.

**Short answer:** ask to see the report, gather evidence of how you wrote the essay, offer to talk through it, and ask for a decision based on that evidence rather than the score.

## What the number actually measures

An AI detector reads your finished essay and asks one question: how predictable is each word, given the words before it? AI writing tends to be predictable, because predicting the next word is literally how it’s made.

The trouble is that a lot of human writing is predictable too. Clear writing is predictable. Writing by people who learned English as a second language is often predictable: in one Stanford study, popular detectors flagged 61% of essays by non-native English writers as AI.[2] And an essay that follows the five-paragraph template your school taught you is very predictable indeed.

Turnitin knows this. Since July 2024 it hides scores between 1% and 19% behind an asterisk instead of showing a number, because low scores produce more false positives.[3] It also publishes its own error rates: under 1% of whole documents wrongly flagged, for documents it thinks are at least 20% AI,[4] and about 4% of highlighted sentences that are actually human-written.[5]

Under 1% sounds small. It isn’t, at Turnitin’s scale.

- **38,000,000** submissions Turnitin checked in its first six weeks
- **380,000** papers, if just 1% of those were wrongly flagged
- **~750** papers a year that Vanderbilt estimated could be wrongly flagged on its campus alone

The first number is Turnitin’s own.[6] The second is just multiplication. The third is Vanderbilt’s, which did the same sum for its 75,000 papers a year and turned the detector off.[7]

## What to do this week

1. **Don’t panic, and don’t admit to something you didn’t do.** A calm, specific reply works better than an angry one.
2. **Ask for the report.** Which passages were highlighted, and what was the score?
3. **Gather your evidence.** Version history, drafts, notes, outlines, sources, your browser history from the research. Google Docs version history helps, though [it has limits](https://huma.social/blog/google-docs-version-history-proof).
4. **Offer to talk it through.** Explain your argument, your sources and how you got there.
5. **Read the policy.** Check what your school says about AI and about detector results. Many say a score alone isn’t enough.
6. **Ask for a decision based on evidence,** and quote Turnitin’s own disclaimer if you need to.

The strongest of these is the one most people skip: offering to talk. Someone who wrote an essay can explain why paragraph three comes before paragraph four. Someone who didn’t usually can’t. It’s an old-fashioned test, and it’s still the best one. Our guide [Flagged by an AI detector?](https://huma.social/ai-detector-false-positive) goes through each step in more detail.

## Questions

### Can Turnitin be wrong about AI?

Yes. Turnitin says about 4% of highlighted sentences are actually human-written, and its reports say the score shouldn’t be the sole basis for action against a student.

### What does an asterisk mean on Turnitin’s AI score?

Since July 2024, Turnitin shows an asterisk instead of a number for AI scores from 1% to 19%, because low scores are less reliable.

### How do I prove I didn’t use AI?

Show how you wrote it: version history, drafts and notes, or a Huma link that shows every key press, which your teacher can open and check in a browser.
